### 3.1 Voice Out of the Whirlwind, Job 38

**Author:** Unknown  
**Main lens:** Formalist  
**Supporting lens:** Reader-Response

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Formalist**, which looks at how a text’s words and structure create meaning. God’s repeated questions show the limits of human knowledge. For example, God asks where Job was when the earth’s foundations were laid (Job 38.4). I also use **Reader-Response**, which connects the text to my own thoughts and experiences.

**2. Values emphasized**

The passage emphasizes humility, wisdom, and faith. God’s questions invite Job to recognize how much lies beyond his understanding.

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The passage reflects an ancient Hebrew religious view of God as the creator and source of wisdom. It explores how people can face questions they cannot fully answer.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect this to respecting elders and learning from their experience. Listening to others reminds us that we still have things to learn.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

My connection is **SDG 4: Quality Education**, which supports learning throughout life. Humility and curiosity help us ask questions, listen, and continue learning.

**6. Personal reflection**

Accepting my limits gives me more reason to learn. I can be patient when answers are unclear while still trying to understand. This reminds me that admitting I do not know something is a useful starting point. Instead of rushing to an answer, I want to listen, ask better questions, and give myself time to learn from people with more experience.

**Margin note:** Some questions take time. There is still more for me to learn.

**Sources:** *The Bible*, King James Version, Job 38. See source B1 and the Goal 4 link below. The Bikol comparison and SDG connection are personal interpretations.

### 3.2 The Panchatantra

**Traditionally attributed to:** Vishnu Sharma  
**Main lens:** Structuralist  
**Supporting lens:** Archetypal

**1. Literary lens and why**

The **Structuralist lens** looks at repeated patterns in stories. Many tales connect a problem, a choice, and its result. I also use the **Archetypal lens**, which studies familiar character roles, such as clever advisers, loyal friends, and tricksters.

**2. Values emphasized**

Wisdom, cooperation, and friendship stand out. In one story, trapped doves lift a hunter’s net together and fly to a mouse who frees them. Their teamwork and friendship help them survive (*Panchatantra*, book 2).

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The collection reflects the importance of practical learning and leadership in ancient Indian court life. Its opening presents Vishnu Sharma teaching princes through stories, showing how storytelling can prepare future rulers to make wiser choices.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect the animals’ friendship to loyalty among friends and family in Bikol communities. Like the mouse helping the doves, we show loyalty by offering help when someone needs us.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The stories connect to **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**. Their lessons offer a way to discuss responsible leadership, cooperation, and careful decisions that prevent conflicts from growing.

**6. Personal reflection**

What stayed with me is that being clever does not mean handling everything alone. The doves survive because they work together and accept help. I can apply this in group work by listening and recognizing what others can contribute. The stories also remind me to question advice before following it. When I feel pressured, taking a moment to think could prevent a small mistake from becoming a bigger problem.

**Margin note:** Think carefully. Choose good company. Accept help.

**Sources:** Source B2, especially the opening and book 2, and the Goal 16 link below.

### 3.3 The Mahabharata

**Traditionally attributed to:** Vyasa  
**Main lens:** Historical

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Historical**, which connects a work to the beliefs and society where it developed. This helps me understand why family duties, leadership, and moral responsibility shape the characters’ choices.

**2. Values emphasized**

Duty, justice, and family loyalty are important, but they sometimes clash. Before battle, Arjuna hesitates when he sees relatives and teachers among his opponents. His struggle shows how difficult a decision becomes when responsibility and love for family pull in different directions (*Bhagavad Gita*, chapter 1).

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The epic explores **dharma**, meaning moral duty and right conduct. In its ancient Indian setting, people face duties connected to their roles as relatives, rulers, and warriors. The story shows that knowing the right action is not always simple.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect this to balancing strong family ties with doing what is right. Supporting a relative can also mean correcting them respectfully when their actions hurt others.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The conflict connects to **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**. The suffering caused by the war raises questions about responsible leadership, justice, and the cost of struggles for power.

**6. Personal reflection**

The family conflict made me think about the limits of loyalty. Caring about someone should not mean defending everything they do. I also realized how pride and competition can turn a disagreement into something more harmful. In my own relationships, I want to listen before taking sides and admit when I am wrong. Keeping a relationship healthy matters more than proving that I have won an argument.

**Margin note:** Loyalty also means being honest about what is wrong.

**Sources:** Source B3 for the scene in the *Bhagavad Gita*, a section of *The Mahabharata*. Source C1 supports the cultural discussion. See the Goal 16 link below.

### 3.4 The Iliad

**Author:** Homer  
**Main lens:** Psychoanalytic  
**Supporting lens:** Historical

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Psychoanalytic**, which studies how feelings and inner struggles shape behavior. Achilles’ anger after his argument with Agamemnon affects the whole Greek army. I also use the **Historical lens** to understand the poem’s ideas about honor and warfare (*Iliad*, book 1).

**2. Values emphasized**

Compassion, friendship, and family loyalty give the story its emotional strength. Achilles grieves for Patroclus, while Priam risks entering an enemy camp to ask for Hector’s body. Their shared sorrow helps Achilles show compassion (*Iliad*, book 24).

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The poem presents a Greek warrior society that values honor and lasting fame. Yet Priam’s request also shows the importance of family duties and giving the dead a proper burial.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect their shared grief to **pagtinarabangan**, helping one another during hardship. Supporting someone who is suffering can begin with listening and recognizing their pain.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The story connects to **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**. It encourages discussion about leaders’ responsibility for others and how dialogue can interrupt revenge. Achilles’ compassion allows Hector’s family time to mourn, although it does not end the war.

**6. Personal reflection**

Achilles made me think about how anger can narrow my attention until I only see my own pain. When that happens, I may forget how my choices affect others. His meeting with Priam also showed me that compassion takes strength. I want to pause before reacting and listen, even during disagreements. Someone who has hurt me can still be a person who is hurting too.

**Margin note:** Compassion can be an act of strength.

**Sources:** Source B4, books 1 and 24, and the Goal 16 link below.

### 3.5 The Odyssey

**Author:** Homer  
**Main lens:** Archetypal  
**Supporting lens:** Psychoanalytic

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Archetypal**, which studies familiar story patterns, such as a hero facing trials before returning home. The **Psychoanalytic lens** also helps explain how feelings shape decisions. Odysseus’ pride, for example, makes him taunt the Cyclops despite his crew’s warnings (*Odyssey*, book 9).

**2. Values emphasized**

Perseverance, wisdom, and family loyalty guide the journey. Odysseus uses clever plans to survive, but returning to Ithaca remains his goal. His longing for home gives his struggles a purpose beyond fame.

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The poem reflects the importance of home, family, and hospitality in its Greek setting. Welcoming guests creates bonds between households, while mistreating visitors breaks an important social duty.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect the journey to staying strong through hardship with family support. In Bikol families, close relationships can give people a reason to keep going, even when they are far from home.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The ending connects to **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**. After Odysseus kills the suitors, their relatives seek revenge. Athena stops further fighting and establishes peace. This raises a question that still matters today: how can justice prevent another cycle of violence? (*Odyssey*, book 24).

**6. Personal reflection**

I see the journey as a reminder that reaching a goal takes patience and the ability to change plans. Being capable does not mean every decision I make will be wise. Odysseus’ pride shows how wanting recognition can put other people at risk. As I work toward my own goals, I want to stay open to advice and remember why I started. Success would feel less meaningful without people I care about to share it with.

**Margin note:** Keep moving, but remember why you started.

**Sources:** Source B5, books 9 and 24, and the Goal 16 link below. The Butler translation uses Ulysses and Minerva for Odysseus and Athena. The website uses the Greek names consistently.

### 3.6 The Tale of Genji

**Author:** Murasaki Shikibu  
**Main lens:** Psychoanalytic  
**Supporting lens:** Feminist

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Psychoanalytic**, which explores how feelings and desires shape behavior. It helps explain Genji’s longing for affection and the jealousy surrounding his relationships. The **Feminist lens**, which examines gender and power, also helps me question how much freedom women have.

**2. Values emphasized**

I read the story as showing the need for care, responsibility, and respect through the harm caused when these are missing. In the rainy-night conversation, Genji listens as men discuss the ideal wife and suggest that women should quietly tolerate men’s affairs. Their expectations place an unfair burden on women (“The Broom Tree,” chapter 2).

**3. Connection to its civilization**

The novel presents Japan’s Heian court, where social rank and court customs shape relationships. Poetry, beautiful writing, and seasonal settings are also important parts of this world. Its elegant surroundings exist alongside emotional struggles.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

My connection is the importance of considering other people’s feelings and dignity. In relationships within Bikol communities, respect should include listening and allowing others to express their needs.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The story connects to **SDG 5: Gender Equality**. The unequal expectations placed on women invite discussion about their freedom to make choices, express hurt, and receive equal respect in relationships.

**6. Personal reflection**

The story made me separate receiving attention from receiving lasting care. Someone can offer admiration without taking responsibility for the feelings they create. For me, care should be seen in consistent actions, honest communication, and respect for another person’s choices. I want to remember this in my own relationships. Feeling noticed may be comforting, but feeling heard and respected matters more over time.

**Margin note:** Lasting care is shown through consistent actions.

**Sources:** Source B6 for the chapter 2 excerpt, C2 for court culture and art, and the Goal 5 link below. The characters' opinions in the conversation are not automatically the author's own beliefs.

### 3.7 Les Misérables

**Author:** Victor Hugo  
**Main lens:** Marxist  
**Supporting lens:** Historical

**1. Literary lens and why**

My main lens is **Marxist**, which examines how poverty, wealth, and social power affect people’s lives. It helps explain why hard work alone cannot solve every character’s struggles. The **Historical lens** also connects these experiences to the novel’s nineteenth-century French setting.

**2. Values emphasized**

Compassion, justice, and forgiveness shape Valjean’s journey. When he steals the bishop’s silver, the bishop protects him from arrest and gives him two candlesticks as well. This act offers him a chance to change instead of another punishment (*Les Misérables*, vol. 1, book 2, chapter 12).

**3. Connection to its civilization**

Hugo presents a French society where poverty and strict punishment can keep people trapped. After leaving prison, Valjean is refused shelter because his passport identifies him as a former prisoner. His sentence has ended, but social rejection continues.

**4. Connection to Bikol culture**

I connect the bishop’s forgiveness to giving someone another chance after a mistake. In my view, supporting change is one way Bikol families and communities can show care without ignoring responsibility.

**5. Contemporary issue or SDG**

The novel connects to **SDG 1: No Poverty**. Hunger, insecure work, and limited opportunities still prevent people from building stable lives. The story encourages discussion about access to basic needs and support for people facing hardship.

**6. Personal reflection**

Valjean’s story reminds me that a person’s past does not tell me everything about who they can become. Before judging someone, I should try to understand the situation behind their actions. The bishop’s kindness also stayed with me because he offered help when rejection would have been easier. I may not be able to change someone’s whole life, but listening, offering support, or giving them another chance could help them move forward.

**Margin note:** Leave room for people to change.

**Sources:** Source B7, especially vol. 1, book 2, chapters 1, 3, and 12, and the Goal 1 link below.

## 4. Favorite Masterpiece

**The Odyssey by Homer**

*Staying focused on the journey ahead*

The Odyssey follows Odysseus as he struggles to return home after the Trojan War. Along the way, he faces dangers, distractions, and the consequences of his own decisions.

What interests me most is that Odysseus is capable but imperfect. His cleverness helps him escape the Cyclops, yet his pride makes him taunt the creature afterward, putting his crew in danger. This moment shows that ability needs to be guided by self-control (*Odyssey*, book 9).

As a fourth-year Computer Science student preparing to start my career, I connect with the need to keep moving toward a goal. Perseverance helps me continue when progress feels slow. Discipline helps me stay consistent, while self-control helps me manage distractions and outside pressure.

The story also reminds me to manage my feelings and consider how my actions affect others. Reaching a goal should not come at the cost of ignoring people around me.

This is why The Odyssey is my favorite. Its journey helps me think about my own future. I want to stay patient, listen to advice, and keep my priorities clear as I take the next steps in my life.

**Margin note:** Perseverance. Discipline. Self-control.

**Source:** B5, book 9.

## 5. Summary of Learning

**What I Learned Along the Way**

Great Books helped me see that stories from different places and periods can share concerns about family, suffering, duty, and belonging. At the same time, understanding a work’s culture helped me make sense of choices that first seemed unfamiliar.

Three literary lenses were especially helpful. The **Psychoanalytic lens** helped me understand characters’ feelings and decisions. The **Historical lens** connected their actions to the society around them. **Reader-Response** helped me connect the works to my own thoughts and experiences.

My biggest improvement was learning to look beyond what happens in a story. In The Iliad, for example, Achilles is more than a strong warrior. His anger, grief, and compassion reveal how people can hurt others and still find a moment of understanding.

I now pay more attention to why characters act as they do and what their choices reveal. Literature has become a way for me to understand people and reflect on my own decisions.

**Margin note:** Look beyond what happened. Ask why it matters.

**Source for the literary example:** B4, books 1 and 24.

## 6. Personal Reflection

**Looking Back, Moving Forward**

I enjoyed literature in high school, but Great Books helped bring that interest back. At first, I expected the course to involve a lot of reading. Ma’am Raquel made it enjoyable, and learning from her and my classmates helped me appreciate what stories can offer.

My biggest challenges were understanding unfamiliar cultural backgrounds and explaining deeper meanings instead of only summarizing events. Breaking the works into smaller parts helped. I also returned to important scenes and thought about why the characters acted as they did. This taught me to slow down and check my understanding.

**Reader-Response influenced my reading the most.** This approach connects a text to the reader’s thoughts and experiences. It helped me see why a story matters to my own life, especially when I connected The Odyssey to preparing for my career. The Psychoanalytic lens, which explores feelings and inner struggles, and the Historical lens, which connects a work to its society and beliefs, also helped me understand the characters and their world.

Across the seven works, I noticed how pride, fear, loyalty, and the need for care influence people. Different cultures express these struggles differently, but many of the feelings remain familiar.

As I prepare to begin my career, I want to carry these lessons with me. The Odyssey especially reminds me to stay patient, disciplined, and focused. I also want to manage my feelings and understand people before judging them. In my future work, being capable should go together with listening, continuing to learn, and considering how my choices affect others.

**Closing note:** There is more to learn, and I want to keep going.
